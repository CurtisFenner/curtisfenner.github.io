Colorimetry is the scientific and quantitative study of color.

# Spectral Colors

Light is made up of **photons**. Each photon has a **wavelength**. Humans eyes
can detect photons that have a wavelength between about 380nm and
700nm[^nasa_range].

[^nasa_range]: https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/

Humans do this using 3 types of "cone" light receptors in our eyes. Each cone is
receptive to only certain wavelengths:
* "long" cones (for "long" wavelengths: red)
* "medium" cones (green),
* "short" cones (blue)

Precise measurements have been made that quantitatively describe how sensitive
each cone is to each frequency of light.

# White Balance

These let us
compare activations of a single type of cone for different wavelengths, but
they don't directly tell us how the different types of cones relate to each
other to make colors.

To do that, we first have to define a **white point**: the mix of L, M, and S
responses that we will consider "colorless". In real life, we do that
with context: in a room with "warm" incandescent lighting, we still consider
plain copy paper to be "white" (colorless) despite the yellowish light bouncing
off of it. Our eyes will adjust what we consider to be "colorless" based on how
the environment we are in is lit.

Therefore, we typically associate _white points_ with _different types of
illumination_.

## Color Temperature

When things get really hot, they start to glow. You can see this in a campfire,
where the coals at the bottom of the fire glow a deep, dim red when they are
cooling off. When they get hotter, they will start to glow orange, and then
yellow, and then white. This has actually been known by smiths and ceramicists
for millennia; they could judge whether the kiln or forge was the right
temperature from observing the color of the coals.

It turns out there is a scientific explanation for this phenomenon, called
black-body radiation. Curiously, it predicts that more or less _any_ material
will glow _the same color_ when heated to the same temperature. Coal, plastic,
glass, wood -- when heated to 3000 K (2700 C or 5000 F), all will glow the same
shade of orange.

For this reason, we can identify points on the spectrum with a _temperature_,
which is typically done in Kelvin (K). This "temperature" refers literally to
the temperature that you would have to heat a brick to in order to glow that
color.

A typical incandescent lightbulb's filament glows at about 2500 K (both in terms
of literal temperature, and in terms of color temperature!)

The surface of the sun is roughly 6000 K; thus, in space, the color temperature
of sunlight is about 6000 K. On earth, sunlight is filtered through the
atmosphere, which can dramatically change its color. The
"correlated color temperature" of sunlight thus varies between about 5000K to
7000K, with 6500K being typical for overcast noon daylight.

## Standard Illuminants

The typical "default" white point is called the
[**D65 white point**](https://doi.org/10.25039/CIE.DS.hjfjmt59).
It corresponds roughly to the color of typical mid-day sun.
The "D" refers to "Daylight"; the "65" refers to
having a **color temperature** of about 6500 K.

# Gamma

Most computer displays nowadays use "24-bit color". This assigns 8-bits to each
of the red, green, and blue channels, giving 256 different possible brightnesses
for each red/green/blue phosphor.

The brightnesses of these levels are not equally spaced, because small
increases in light from a dark gray are much more noticeable than the same
increase in brightness added to a light gray.

Mapping the 256 different levels to an actual amount of light to emit is called
[gamma correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_correction).
