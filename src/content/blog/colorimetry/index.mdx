Colorimetry is the scientific and quantitative study of color.

# Spectral Colors

Light is made up of **photons**. Each photon has a **wavelength**. Humans eyes
can detect photons that have a wavelength between about 380nm and
700nm[^nasa_range].

[^nasa_range]: https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/

Humans do this using 3 types of light receptors in our eyes, called **cones**.
Each cone is receptive to only certain wavelengths:
* "long" cones are sensitive to longer wavelengths of light (reds and yellows)
* "medium" cones are sensitive to medium wavelengths (yellows and greens)
* "short" cones are sensitive to short wavelengths (blues and teals)

Precise measurements have been made that quantitatively describe how sensitive
each cone is to each frequency of light.

# CIE XYZ

In 1931, the CIE XYZ color space was published. XYZ gives quantitative
predictions about how humans perceive different light spectra. However, the
"X", "Y", and "Z" components don't directly correspond to anything fundamentally
happening in our eyes.

Rather, XYZ is constructed to be a _linear basis_ for the "real" LMS color space
being detected by human eyes. At the time XYZ was constructed, it wasn't
possible to confirm the particular curves for the L, M, and S cones; however,
it _was_ possible to accurately construct fictitious linear _combinations_ of
those.

XYZ was roughly constructed through these principles:
* X(&lambda;), Y(&lambda;), and Z(&lambda;) should be linear combinations of
  the experimentally measured color-matching-functions
  $\bar r(\lambda)$, $\bar g(\lambda)$, and $\bar b(\lambda)$.
* X(&lambda;), Y(&lambda;), and Z(&lambda;)
  should always be non-negative across the entire visible spectrum
	* The color matching functions derived experimentally necessarily
	  had negative values on their range
* Y(&lambda;) should be fixed to the **luminous efficiency function**. This was a
  previously measured curve that answered how "bright" each frequency of light
  appeared to observers. (it's harder to read by blue light than
  red light of the same wattage; similarly it's even easier to read by green
  light).
* X/Y/Z should be scaled so that the _equal energy illuminant_ would have a
  chromaticity of x=y=z=1/3 (X=1, Y=1, Z=1)

# White Balance

How do the distinct qualia of the L-, M-, and S-cones get related to each other?

To understand whether or not a combination is "pink" or "green" or "orange", we
compare them to some relative combination of L/M/S that we consider "colorless"
or "gray".

In real life, we do that with context: in a room with "warm" incandescent
lighting, we still consider plain copy paper to be "white" (colorless) despite
the yellowish light bouncing off of it. Our eyes will adjust what we consider to
be "colorless" based on how the environment we are in is lit.

Therefore, we typically associate _white points_ with _different types of
illumination_.

## Color Temperature

When things get really hot, they start to glow. You can see this in a campfire,
where the coals at the bottom of the fire glow a deep, dim red when they are
starting to heat up. When they get hotter, they will start to glow orange, and
then yellow, and then white. This has actually been known by smiths and
ceramicists for millennia; they could judge whether the kiln or forge had
reached (or overshot) the desired temperature just from observing the color
the coals.

It turns out there is a scientific explanation for this phenomenon, called
black-body radiation. Curiously, it predicts that more or less _any_ material
will glow _the same color_ when heated to the same temperature. Coal, plastic,
glass, wood -- when heated to 3000 K (2700 C or 5000 F), all will glow the same
shade of orange.

For this reason, we can identify points on the spectrum with a _temperature_,
which is typically done in Kelvin (K). This "temperature" refers literally to
the temperature that you would have to heat a brick to in order to glow that
color.

A typical incandescent lightbulb's filament glows at about 2500 K (both in terms
of literal temperature, and in terms of color temperature!)

The surface of the sun is roughly 6000 K; thus, in space, the color temperature
of sunlight is about 6000 K. On earth, sunlight is filtered through the
atmosphere, which can dramatically change its color. The
"correlated color temperature" of sunlight thus varies between about 5000K to
7000K, with 6500K being typical for overcast noon daylight.

> Note that the common understanding of "warm lighting" being red or orange,
> and "cool lighting" being blue or teal, is _opposite_ the literal color
> temperature. So don't confuse "warm" lighting with "high color temperature"!
>
> This inversion is natural because in real life, we don't really encounter
> fires that are particularly hot, in terms of color-temperature.
> Until around 5000 K, black-body radiation is decidedly red/orange;
> only after about 6000 K does it start to leave the "warm tones" range of hues.
>
> However, a fire that hot would also be so bright that you could not really
> appreciate its hue. Therefore correlating greenish, tealish, or blueish
> lighting with a "color temperature" is really a theoretical exercise. Actually
> producing these colors through black-body radiation is totally impractical,
> even in a laboratory.
>
> Most colors and most spectra are not black body radiation!

The "D" refers to "Daylight"; the "65" refers to

# sRGB

**sRGB** is an old (1999) and commonly supported color space, originally
developed to standardize color on CRT monitors. Even today, it is the most
common way to interpret "RGB" color specifications; however, because of its
origin in fairly old technology, it is somewhat limited in which colors it can
accurately reproduce.

## sRGB (ITU Rec. 709) Color Primaries

sRGB was defined in the era of cathode-ray-tube monitors. These monitors work by
energizing red, green, and blue _phosphors_, each of which is a mix of special
chemicals which glow with a specific spectrums of light.

The recommended target colors for these phosphors was defined in
[ITU-R Recommendation 709](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rec._709), commonly
just called **Rec. 709** (1993).

Rather than define the full spectrum of phosphors, Rec. 709 just defines their
**chromaticities** using CIE 1931 2-degree xyY.

## Gamma

Most computer displays nowadays use "24-bit color". This assigns 8-bits to each
of the red, green, and blue channels, giving 256 different possible brightnesses
for each red/green/blue phosphor.

The brightnesses of these levels are not equally spaced, because small
increases in light from a dark gray are much more noticeable than the same
increase in brightness added to a light gray.

The process of associating each of the 256 possible levels with a specific
amount of light to emit is called
[gamma correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_correction).
